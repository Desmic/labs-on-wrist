Labs on the Wrist

    The life between appointments

    A doctor might see someone with epilepsy for twenty minutes every few months. Everything else happens out of sight. We are building an affordable wearable and a shared health timeline to help close that gap, carefully and in the open.

    Start the story Read the two-minute version

    The two-minute version

    What we're building
    A low-cost wrist device, a phone app and a timeline that a person can choose to share with their doctor. The device notices movement and pulse; the person adds what they felt; the doctor adds test results and context.
    Why
    Care decisions often rest on what someone can remember from the last few months. For people with epilepsy, many seizures happen alone or during sleep, and studies show diaries miss a large share of them.
    What comes first
    Safety. Fall alerts and a physical SOS button that works even without the internet. Seizure alerts come later, only after clinical testing.
    The big research question
    Can a small, cheap set of sensors carry enough information to answer specific questions that normally need expensive hospital tests? Sometimes yes, sometimes no. We want to find out which is which, honestly.

    This is an early-stage concept and research programme. Nothing here is a medical device or a diagnosis. Aarav, the person in this story, is fictional, and every chart on this site uses made-up data.

    Chapter 1

    A life like any other

    This is Aarav. He's 29 and works as a structural engineer. He makes chai on the balcony before work, visits building sites, and plays cricket with friends on Thursday evenings.

    He has plans. A promotion he's working towards. A trip to the hills with his sister in the spring.

    Like everyone else, he has a life he wants to live.

    Chapter 2

    Living with uncertainty

    Aarav has epilepsy. Most days, nothing happens. But some mornings he wakes up tired, with aching muscles and a sore tongue, and no memory of the night.

    Did something happen? He lives alone. Nobody saw. He goes to work anyway, a little less sure of himself.

    Not everyone with epilepsy lives like this. Every person's experience is different. But not knowing is something many people share.

    For him, uncertainty is part of every day.

    Chapter 3

    The missing story

    Every few months, Aarav sees his neurologist. She asks careful questions. How many events since last time? How has he been sleeping? Any side effects since the dose change?

    He tries to remember. "Maybe two? I think I slept okay." Three months of life, squeezed into a twenty-minute conversation.

    This isn't a failing of doctors. It's a limit of the information that reaches them.

    His doctor sees moments. He lives everything in between.

    Chapter 4

    Fragments

    The story does exist, but in pieces. A prescription slip. An EEG report from March. A note on his phone: "felt strange on the bus." A sleep app he stopped opening. A message from his mother: "you sounded confused this morning."

    Each piece holds part of the picture. None of them are connected, and none of them are in the room when decisions are made.

    Putting things side by side in time doesn't prove that one caused another. But it helps the right questions get asked.

    What if these pieces could be connected?

    Chapter 5

    A different approach

    Same morning. Same balcony, same chai. This time Aarav wears a simple band on his wrist.

    Quietly, it keeps a record: how he moved, his pulse, when he slept. When he feels something odd, he taps the band or adds a note in his own words.

    The record isn't perfect. There are gaps when the band was charging or he forgot to wear it. We show those gaps rather than hide them.

    What you're seeing is our design concept. The device is at the prototype-planning stage.

    What if we could begin to understand the person, not just the appointment?

    Chapter 6

    Making the invisible measurable

    Inside the band: a motion sensor that feels movement and rotation, an optical sensor that watches tiny changes in blood flow under the skin, a skin-temperature sensor, and possibly a sensor for skin conductance. Plus a small processor, memory, Bluetooth, a battery, a vibration motor and a big physical SOS button.

    It's important to be clear about what a wrist device can and can't know.

    Measures directly
    Movement, rotation, changes in skin blood volume, skin temperature, a button press.
    Estimates
    Pulse rate, sleep timing, activity, and candidate events such as a possible fall.
    Cannot know
    Brain activity, blood chemistry, what an MRI shows, or a diagnosis.

    The first prototype keeps it simple: motion sensor, SOS button, vibration and a phone link. Other sensors are added only if testing shows they're worth their cost and battery.

    Chapter 7

    The whole person

    The human body does not work as a collection of separate parts.

    Sleep affects seizures. Medication affects sleep. Stress shows up in pulse and in mood. So the record is organised around the person over time, not around one organ or one specialist.

    Some layers come from the band, some from Aarav himself, some from his doctors. Each one keeps track of where it came from and how sure we are about it.

    Turn the layers on and off. Gaps are times with no data. There's no single "health score". A number like that would hide more than it shows.

    Chapter 8

    Checking against the real thing

    A cheap sensor is only useful if we know how well it agrees with the tests doctors trust. So we compare them side by side, at the same moment.

    Pulse from the band against an ECG. Movement events against video-EEG recordings in hospital. Sleep timing against a sleep lab. Each comparison answers one narrow question.

    Then we test on people the system has never seen, because a model that only works on the people it learned from isn't useful. What we learn feeds back into the next version of the hardware.

    Measure. Compare. Validate. Learn. Repeat.

    Chapter 9

    Labs on the wrist

    Hospitals measure a lot: blood panels, scans, brain recordings. But a specific decision often depends on a much smaller part of that information. Desmic's founding question is whether a few cheap, continuous measurements can preserve enough of it, for one clearly defined question at a time.

    It's not about putting an MRI scanner in a watch. For some questions a wrist band may be enough. For others, the information simply isn't there, and the honest answer is "get the scan".

    Measurements to include

    Every number in this experiment is made up to illustrate the idea. These are not real accuracy figures or prices.

    In research terms, for each clinical question T we look for the set of measurements S with the lowest cost that still keeps the error below a threshold a clinician agrees is safe:

    S* = arg minS ⊆ F C(S)  subject to  RT(fS) ≤ εT

    Cost includes money, battery, comfort and false alarms, not just the price of a sensor. If no affordable set meets the threshold, the system must say "I can't tell" and point back to proper clinical testing.

    How little must we measure to understand enough?

    Chapter 10

    The next appointment

    Same clinic, same doctor, same twenty minutes. This time Aarav has chosen to share his timeline with her.

    She can see two possible night-time events the band flagged, marked as unconfirmed. Four days when the band wasn't worn. Shorter sleep after the dose change. And Aarav's own notes, in his words.

    She still decides what it means. The band doesn't diagnose anything. Aarav controls who sees his record and for how long.

    A better-informed conversation about the same human being.

    Chapter 11

    Beyond epilepsy

    Many people live with health that changes between visits: older adults at risk of falls, children whose parents worry at night, neurodivergent people who want support with routines and sensory overload, and people managing long-term conditions.

    We're doing this in order, and we'll say clearly what stage each part is at.

    • Building first Fall alerts and a physical SOS that works offline. A diary and timeline people can share with their doctor.
    • Research Alerts for convulsive seizures, tested against hospital video-EEG before anyone relies on them. Sleep and daily functioning. Labs on the wrist.
    • Exploring later Filtering flashing light on screens or glasses for the small share of people whose seizures are triggered by light. Unproven, and treated as a separate safety study.

    Children come with extra care: separate ethics approval, parental consent and the child's own agreement, and designs made for them rather than shrunk from adult versions.

    Chapter 12

    Why we build

    Back on the balcony. Chai. A Thursday match later. The band is there, but it isn't the point.

    The future of healthcare should give us more freedom to live, not more reasons to think about being ill.

    The original vision and research ideas behind this project come from Desmic (Kumar Shashank). We're looking for clinicians, researchers, engineers and people with lived experience who want to help build it well.

    What's true today, and what we're testing

    We separate what is already known from what we hope is true. If you're a clinician or researcher and something here is wrong or out of date, we want to hear it.

    Already established by others

    • Around 50 million people worldwide live with epilepsy. WHO fact sheet
    • When people keep seizure diaries, many seizures go unrecorded. In one hospital video-EEG study, patients missed about half of their recorded seizures, and most of those that began during sleep. Hoppe et al., Archives of Neurology, 2007
    • Wearable devices are recommended, as an aid to care, for detecting generalised tonic-clonic and focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, not all seizure types. It's a weak recommendation, and whether alarms improve outcomes is still uncertain. ILAE/IFCN clinical practice guideline, 2021
    • A smartwatch ECG study could flag weak heart pumping that is normally found on a heart scan. Of 421 people with a scan to compare against, only 16 had the condition, so it's a promising early result rather than proof. Attia et al., Nature Medicine, 2022
    • Only a small share of people with epilepsy, around 3 in 100, have seizures triggered by flashing light. Epilepsy Action
    • Smartwatches and rings that claim to measure blood sugar without a needle should not be used for that purpose. US FDA safety communication

    What we're testing

    • Whether a simple wrist device and shared timeline make clinic conversations better, without adding work for doctors.
    • Which reduced sets of sensors are good enough for which specific clinical questions, and which questions they can't answer.
    • Whether personal baselines help spot meaningful change in one person over time.
    • How to design alerts that caregivers can trust, with false alarms kept low enough to live with.

    What we won't claim

    • That a wrist band detects every seizure or predicts seizures.
    • That it replaces MRI, EEG, blood tests or doctors.
    • That it can diagnose ADHD, autism, depression or any condition.
    • That anything here is clinically validated today.

    How we'll work

    • Safety alerts run on the device and phone. They never depend on a cloud AI or an internet connection.
    • The person owns their record and decides who sees it. Research participation is always optional and separate.
    • Every number keeps its source, how it was measured and how confident we are. A guess is never shown as a fact.
    • No real health data is collected through this website.
    • We publish what didn't work, not just what did.
